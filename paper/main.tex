\begin{abstract}
On ambiguous items, abstention can be appropriate. A lower bias score may
reflect more abstention, not a change in preferences. Using an exact
identity implied by BBQ's metric, we decompose 32 published method--model
results: a median 80.4\% of the reported improvement is abstention, not reduced
bias, committed-answer bias \emph{worsened} in 7 pairs, and the identified
set widened in 31. First-party, on six checkpoints and eleven BBQ categories,
abstention is a property of the checkpoint (83\% of variance) and the
stereotype-aligned rate a property of category (68\%), so the score ranks
checkpoints largely by abstention ($\rho=-0.83$), surviving reworded
prompts and extending to twelve checkpoints and six languages, with one
informative exception explained by the identity itself. Abstainers still
lean toward the stereotype (56--64\%). The
natural remedy, forced choice checked for agreement, is unsound in its
plug-in form: under a resampled null it falsely rejects about 21\% of items
at 16 draws, unimproved through 4{,}096 draws. An exact-binomial correction
yields empirical false-positive rates of at most 0.05\%. Applied to
138{,}240 generations, the certificate separates instrument failures from
response-policy effects. For Qwen2.5, the recovery gap strengthens under a
cleaner instrument the certificate clears. Both tools generalize beyond BBQ
and WinoGender.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bias benchmarks for large language models routinely allow a model to decline
to answer (by selecting an \textsc{unknown} option, saying ``cannot be
determined,'' or otherwise abstaining) and then report a score computed on
what remains. This is standard on BBQ's ambiguous-context items
\citep{parrish2022bbq} and on WinoGender-style coreference probes
\citep{rudinger2018gender}. It is also a specific threat to validity: if
abstention is not independent of the disposition being measured, the reported
score confounds what the model prefers with how willing it is to say so, and
no amount of additional data closes the gap. \citet{manski1989anatomy} gave
the general form of this problem for missing outcomes decades before it
became relevant here.

The concern is easy to state and easy to dismiss as theoretical. Our first
contribution is to show it is neither. BBQ's ambiguous bias score is defined
so that it is \emph{multiplied} by the complement of the abstention rate.
Since on ambiguous items the gold answer is the \textsc{unknown} option,
that complement is exactly the rate at which the model commits to an answer.
An intervention that only teaches a model to abstain more therefore drives
the reported score toward zero while telling us strictly less about the
model. Applying this decomposition to every method--model pair in the main
results table of a recent debiasing paper, we find a median 80.4\% of
reported improvement is this mechanical effect, and that the evaluation's
identified set widened in 31 of 32 cases: the reported success and the loss
of identification are the same event (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). We then establish the phenomenon
independently of any published harness, first-party on six checkpoints
across all eleven BBQ categories (\S\ref{sec:firstparty}). Abstention turns
out to be a property of the checkpoint and disposition a property of the
social category, so comparing checkpoints, which is what a leaderboard does,
ranks them substantially by abstention.

\input{figures/fig_teaser}

If reported scores are compromised this way, the obvious response is to
re-elicit under a forced-choice policy, or against some instrument that
removes the model's ability to decline, and then check whether the
conditions agree. Our second contribution asks whether \emph{that} check is
itself sound, and finds that the natural implementation is not. We pose
agreement as a linear-programming compatibility certificate over an
arbitrary number of elicitation conditions (Figure~\ref{fig:method} maps both
analyses). Evaluated on plug-in proportions, it rejects a null with no
cross-condition structure 21.3\% of the time at 16 draws, and calibrated
against a null resampled from our own panel, more draws do not fix this: the
rate plateaus at 21--23\% out to 4{,}096 draws. An exact-binomial version has
a false-positive rate of at most 0.1\% at every draw count tested.

We run both versions on six instruction\hy{}tuned checkpoints from four
families, on all 480 eligible WinoGender items, using a
refusal\hy{}direction ablation \citep{arditi2024refusal} as a
refusal\hy{}ablated comparator. A per-model null explains 83--94\% of each
checkpoint's naive rejections. The corrected certificate isolates 29
violations: 25 implicate the comparator itself, including on a checkpoint
that never once abstains, so there is no refusal to remove, 3 are
consistent with genuine response\hy{}policy effects, and 1 is a
forced-condition outlier.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item An exact decomposition of BBQ ambiguous scores into coverage and
  disposition, applied to 32 published method--model pairs and replicated
  first-party on six checkpoints across eleven categories, showing that
  abstention lives in the checkpoint and disposition in the category, that
  abstainers still lean toward the stereotype, and that this survives
  rewording and extends to twelve checkpoints (2024--2026) and six languages
  (\S\ref{sec:decomposition}, \S\ref{sec:firstparty}, \S\ref{sec:multilingual}).
\item A compatibility certificate for $Z$ elicitation conditions posed as a
  linear program, with a necessary-dependence bound $\gamma^*$
  (\S\ref{sec:certificate}).
\item A calibration showing persistent false positives for the plug-in
  certificate under the tested null and sampling budgets, an exact-binomial
  correction, and a per-model null for assessing aggregate incompatibility
  counts (\S\ref{sec:calibration}).
\item Application to six checkpoints, including a taxonomy that distinguishes
  instrument failure from response-policy effects, and evidence that
  abstention leans toward unnamed referents in every checkpoint that abstains
  (\S\ref{sec:models}).
\end{enumerate}

All three tools are benchmark-agnostic. The identity holds for any score of
the form coverage times committed-answer bias, which covers BBQ and every
benchmark that adopts its ambiguous-context metric, and the certificate and
its calibration take any panel of elicitation conditions by answer categories,
whatever produced it. We demonstrate them on BBQ and WinoGender, the standard
selective-answer bias benchmarks with public items. Our critique targets a
metric definition and what can be concluded from it, not the interventions'
authors, who report accuracy and bias score exactly as the benchmark
specifies.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Selective non-response as a confound.}
A cluster of recent work converges on the concern that a model's willingness
to answer is itself a confound in fairness evaluation: desirability-matched
graded forced choice with IRT-estimated latent traits
\citep{okada2026desirability}, clinical-style validity scales for
metacognitive self-report \citep{cacioli2026validity}, an IRT-fitted
``refusal strictness'' factor across 192 safety benchmarks
\citep{fonsecarivera2026irt}, implicit association testing
\citep{bai2025implicit}, list-experiment elicitation of concealed preferences
\citep{chupilkin2026hidden}, guardrail-agnostic probing via image-conveyed
demographics \citep{hirota2026guardrail}, activation steering
\citep{himelstein2026silenced}, and large correspondence-audit designs
\citep{gao2026hire}. Closest to our concern, \citet{gan2026biaxisbias} find
17.1\% of model--statement pairs change their forced-choice selection under
five equivalent wordings alone. UnQover \citep{li2020unqover} established the
forced-choice-only baseline this literature departs from, and
\citet{manduru2026vacuous} name the failure mode ``vacuous neutrality.'' This
body of work establishes the phenomenon. What has been missing is how much of
the published record it accounts for, and whether the natural remedy is
sound. We supply both.

\paragraph{Partial identification.} Given a non-response rate $p$ and an
answered-only alignment $q$, the sharp interval for population alignment is
$[(1-p)q,\ (1-p)q+p]$, assuming disclosed answers reveal the target outcome
and leaving undisclosed outcomes unrestricted \citep{manski1989anatomy}.
Interpreting this target as forced-choice behaviour additionally requires
that forcing preserve answers on disclosed cases.
\citet{horowitz1995identification} extend this to contaminated rather than
merely missing data, the closer analogy for selective disclosure. We use
these bounds as the identification backbone of \S\ref{sec:decomposition}.

\paragraph{Interpretability as an instrument.} Refusal in open-weight chat
models is mediated by a single residual-stream direction
\citep{arditi2024refusal}, which we use to build a refusal-ablated forced
condition, testing rather than assuming its validity: \S\ref{sec:models}
shows the certificate detecting where it fails and that conclusions hold when
it is replaced.

\paragraph{Evaluation data.} We use BBQ \citep{parrish2022bbq} for
\S\ref{sec:decomposition} and WinoGender
\citep{rudinger2018gender,rudinger2018winogender} for \S\ref{sec:models},
noting the template defects catalogued by WinoPron
\citep{gautam2024winopron}.

\section{Reported improvements decompose into coverage and disposition}
\label{sec:decomposition}

\subsection{The identity}

BBQ defines its ambiguous-context bias score as
$s_{\text{AMB}} = (1 - \text{acc}) \cdot s_{\text{DIS}}$
\citep{parrish2022bbq} (top row of Figure~\ref{fig:method}), motivating the scaling as reflecting that a biased
answer ``is more harmful if it happens more often.'' On ambiguous items the
gold answer \emph{is} the \textsc{unknown} option, so $\text{acc}$ is
precisely the abstention rate $p$, and writing $c = 1-p$ for the rate at
which the model commits to a group answer, and $b$ for the directional bias
among committed answers,
\[
s_{\text{AMB}} = c \cdot b .
\]
Any reported change therefore decomposes \emph{exactly}, with no modeling
assumptions and no access to per-item data:
\[
\underbrace{s_1 - s_0}_{\text{reported}} =
\underbrace{b_0\,(c_1 - c_0)}_{\text{coverage}} +
\underbrace{c_1\,(b_1 - b_0)}_{\text{disposition}} .
\]
The second term captures a change in committed-answer bias, which may
reflect both changes in responses and changes in the composition of answered
items. The allocation between terms follows a decomposition convention and is not a
unique causal attribution (symmetric alternative in Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}). The first term says the intervention moved
mass into the abstain option, widening the Manski identified set for the
underlying preference from width $p_0$ to $p_1$. Holding committed-answer bias fixed, increasing abstention moves
the score toward zero while widening the identified interval. This
establishes a mechanical link, not an equivalence between every score
improvement and a loss of identification.

\subsection{Applied to 32 published results}


We apply the decomposition to every method--model pair in Table 11(a) of
\citet{bae2025decap}, a 2025 debiasing paper reporting accuracy and bias score
on the ambiguous split for four interventions (Self-Debiasing, Def-1, Def-2,
DeCAP) across eight models, each against its own \textsc{Base} row
(Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition} and Table~\ref{tab:decomposition},
Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}). We chose it because it reports both quantities the
identity needs under one harness.

All 32 pairs report a reduction in bias score. Across these 32 pairs from a
single study, the coverage component accounts for a median 80.4\% of the
reported score reduction under our convention (65.3\% under a symmetric one,
Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}: the share is convention-dependent, the direction
is not). In 7
pairs the committed-answer bias $b$ moved in the \emph{wrong} direction, so the
model became more biased among the answers it actually gave, while the
reported score improved because rising abstention more than compensated. In 31
of 32 pairs the identified set widened, on average from 0.21 to 0.61, so the
evaluation now pins the disposition only to an interval covering more than half
the unit range.

The clearest single case is Def-2 on FLAN-T5 (3B) (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}). The reported ambiguous
bias score falls from 27.52 to 5.63, an 80\% reduction. The recovered
committed-answer bias moves from 51.21 to 51.18, unchanged to four
significant figures and, given two-decimal rounding, indistinguishable from
zero in sign (Appendix~\ref{app:decomp}). Abstention rises from 46.3\% to
89.0\%. The
intervention did not detectably change what the model prefers when it
answers. It changed how often it answers, and the metric rewards that as if
it were debiasing. This does not show the interventions are worthless: an
intervention that induces appropriate abstention on genuinely ambiguous items
may well be desirable, and BBQ's authors motivate the scaling deliberately. It
shows that the reported number cannot distinguish that case from the case
where nothing about the model's preference changed.

\subsection{First-party replication across four families}
\label{sec:firstparty}

Cross-paper replication should be cheap, since the decomposition needs only two
published numbers per pair. It was not: of three further papers checked, BBQ's
own reports the ambiguous
split only in unlabelled figures. One reports a ``bias score''
\citep{wu2025reasoning} from which $b$ recovers to $[3.1, 10.2]$, impossible for
a quantity bounded in $[-1,1]$. The third reports a custom ``diff-bias'' pooled
across categories. We excluded all three rather than force the identity onto an
incompatible metric, which is itself the argument for the reporting
recommendation in \S\ref{sec:conclusion}, and ran the identity ourselves on the
six checkpoints of \S\ref{sec:models}.

Each checkpoint is scored on 2{,}200 ambiguous items (200 per BBQ category)
under baseline, the same untrained fairness-instruction wording debiasing
papers use, and a forced condition (\textsc{unknown} removed) read via
first-token logits so every item is scored with no refusal-shaped hole in the
estimate. Generation-based forcing agrees closely (max disagreement 0.007,
refusal rate 0.6--4.9\%), cross-validating both. Exact prompts are in
Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.

\emph{Between checkpoints} (Table~\ref{tab:firstparty},
Figure~\ref{fig:identification}), baseline $s_{\text{AMB}}$ spans
5.0$\times$ (0.033--0.164) while the stereotype-aligned rate $\theta$ spans
only 57.6--62.2\%. Each checkpoint's baseline-derived Manski interval contains
its own measured $\theta$ (6 of 6, and 65 of 66 checkpoint--category cells, the one
miss being OLMo-2-Instruct on Age, $\theta=0.615$ against $[0.625, 0.815]$).
The fairness instruction raises abstention everywhere
(41.6--94.8\% $\to$ 86.3--99.7\%) and the resulting score change is
$\ge$89\% mechanical for every checkpoint.

Which quantity drives the score depends on the comparison being made. Across
the six checkpoints the score tracks abstention (Spearman $\rho=-0.83$,
$p=0.042$), but six points cannot say whether it tracks $\theta$
($\rho=-0.37$, bootstrap 95\% CI $[-1.00,+0.80]$). The 66
checkpoint$\times$category cells can (Figure~\ref{fig:variance} and
Appendix~\ref{app:categories}). In the evaluated six-checkpoint, eleven-category grid, checkpoint
differences account for 83\% of the observed abstention variance (against
13\% for category), while category differences account for 68\% of the
observed forced-choice alignment variance (against 6\% for checkpoint,
$\theta$ ranging from 0.46 to 0.77 across cells). These descriptive
proportions need not generalize beyond the evaluated checkpoints, categories,
and prompts. The score mixes the
two: standardised regression over the cells gives $\beta=-0.73$ on abstention
(checkpoint-cluster 95\% CI $[-0.80,-0.58]$) and $\beta=+0.43$ on $\theta$
($[+0.37,+0.50]$, $R^2=0.71$), and abstention is unrelated to $\theta$
($\rho=+0.005$). So the score is not uninformative about disposition: over all 66
cells it rises with $\theta$ ($\rho=+0.44$), and within
every one of the six checkpoints it rises with $\theta$ across categories
($\rho$ from $+0.32$ to $+0.75$). But a checkpoint-to-checkpoint comparison,
which is what a leaderboard makes, is driven substantially by the component
that varies most between checkpoints, and that component is abstention.

\paragraph{Robust to rewording, not to level.} Reworded prompts
(Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}) move abstention \emph{levels} a great deal
(OLMo-2-SFT 46\%$\to$19\%, Mistral 74\%$\to$59\%) but not the
\emph{structure}: checkpoint ordering by abstention is identical
($\rho=+1.00$), abstention remains the checkpoint's property (97\% of
variance) and $\theta$ the category's (77\%), the score still tracks
abstention ($\rho=-1.00$) and $\theta$ across cells ($\rho=+0.74$), and
Manski containment is 64 of 66 ($\theta$ cell agreement lower, $\rho=+0.58$).

\paragraph{What abstainers hide.} Forced choice provides a stereotype
-alignment measure on every item, including those receiving an abstention
under the baseline prompt. This measures behaviour under forced choice.
Interpreting it as an underlying preference requires an additional stability
assumption. In every checkpoint under both
prompts, abstainers still lean toward the stereotype: 55.7--64.2\% of their
forced picks are stereotype-aligned, every stratified-bootstrap lower bound
above 50\% (minimum 52.9\%). Abstention withholds a lean rather than
indicating its absence. Abstainers lean slightly \emph{less} than answerers in
all 12 cells (selection odds ratio $\Lambda<1$), but the category-adjusted gap
is significant (within-category permutation $p<0.05$) in only 4: Qwen2.5 under
both prompts, Mistral and Phi-3.5-mini under the original. Instrument validity
also varies: on committed items the forced pick matches the committed one
96--99\% of the time for Qwen2.5 but only 60--65\% for OLMo-2 (original
prompt), so estimates for weaker instruments are conditional on that.

\subsection{Twelve checkpoints, six languages}
\label{sec:multilingual}

The six checkpoints above are all 2024-era English models. We extend both
axes: six checkpoints released August 2025--March 2026
\citep{qwen2025qwen3,gemmateam2026gemma4,abdin2025phi4mini,ibm2025granite4micro,olmoteam2025olmo3,mistralai2026ministral3},
and five further languages, Spanish, Dutch, Turkish, Korean, and Catalan
(Appendix~\ref{app:multilingual} names each dataset and the one matcher
adjustment needed).

\input{tables/tab_multilingual}

Across 12 checkpoints, English is at least as strong as the six-checkpoint
result it replicates ($\rho=-0.96$ versus $-0.83$), and four of five further
languages reproduce it, with the category share weaker in Turkish and Korean.
Catalan is the exception ($\rho=-0.01$). Recovering
$b=s_{\text{AMB}}/(1-\text{abstention})$ shows why
(Appendix~\ref{app:multilingual}): OLMo-2 and Mistral-7B show essentially no
bias in Catalan despite substantial English bias, while Qwen2.5, Phi, and
Granite retain theirs. When $b$ is not roughly uniform across checkpoints,
abstention no longer dominates $(1-p)b$, exactly as
\S\ref{sec:decomposition}'s identity predicts, not a methodological failure.
A single from-scratch dataset cannot separate a Catalan-language effect from
a CaBBQ-construction one.

\section{A compatibility certificate}
\label{sec:certificate}

If answered-only scores are compromised, the natural response is to elicit
the same items under additional conditions, such as forced choice or an
intervention that removes the ability to decline, and check whether the
conditions are mutually consistent (bottom row of Figure~\ref{fig:method}). We
formalize that check.

Let $p_z(k)$ be the unconditional probability that condition
$z \in \{1..Z\}$ produces substantive category $k \in \{1..K\}$. The residual
row mass is abstained or unparsed output. We ask whether one common answer
law, combined with an arbitrary, adversarial, answer-\emph{dependent}
disclosure policy chosen separately per condition, could reproduce every
observed $p_z(\cdot)$ at once. The condition is
\[
\texttt{compatible}(p) \iff \sum_k \max_z\, p_z(k) \le 1 .
\]
When it fails, we compute the sharp minimum probability that the conditions
must genuinely disagree,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\gamma^* = 1 - \max_t\ & \textstyle\sum_k t_k \\[-2pt]
\text{s.t.}\ & \textstyle\sum_k \max(t_k, p_z(k)) \le 1\ \ \forall z,
\end{aligned}
\]
a linear program in $t$. Compatibility is exactly $\gamma^*=0$. We verified
the LP numerically against an independent exponential-size formulation over
joint atoms on randomized panels and against closed forms for $Z=2$ and $K=2$,
and prove both the characterization and the sharpness of $\gamma^*$ in general
in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}. This
generalizes the two-condition bound of \S\ref{sec:decomposition} to arbitrarily
many conditions and provides a per-item test of a common-answer-law model.
Rejection challenges that model, but non-rejection does not establish the
validity of any individual instrument.

\paragraph{What this adds.} The certificate builds on two classical tools, a
Fréchet-type feasibility bound and a minimum-disagreement coupling. The
contribution is the reduction: instrument agreement in selective-answer
evaluation is exactly this feasibility problem, turning an assumption every
forced-choice audit makes implicitly into a per-item statistic, plus the
finite-sample calibration of \S\ref{sec:calibration}, without which the test
misleads at every affordable draw count.

\section{Calibrating the certificate}
\label{sec:calibration}

\input{figures/fig_calibration}

\paragraph{Plug-in false positives persist under the tested null and sampling budgets.}
LLM evaluations run at single- or low-double-digit draw counts. We constructed a
null in which one shared answer law generates all $Z=3$ conditions and each
discloses at its own answer-\emph{independent} rate, so the population panel
is compatible by construction and every rejection is a false positive. Rather
than a convenient parametric family, we resampled the shared answer law and
the per-condition disclosure rates from our own six-checkpoint panel
(\S\ref{sec:models}): disclosure is concentrated near 1 (92.5\% of 8{,}640
(item, condition) rates exceed 0.95) and answer-law skew near 0 or 1 (82.2\%
of 8{,}612 observations lie outside $[0.1,0.9]$). Under this null the plug-in
certificate rejects 21.3\% of the time at 16 draws
(Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}) and, unlike under a generic null, this does
\emph{not} improve with more data: it plateaus at 21--23\% out to 4{,}096
draws, 256$\times$ our budget. The mechanism is structural: with near-total
disclosure the abstained-mass slack that would let \texttt{compatible}
tolerate sampling noise disappears, and the check asks whether three
independent continuous-valued estimates coincide exactly, which finite-sample
noise defeats however large the sample. At the compatibility boundary,
sampling fluctuations can cause rejection even at large budgets. For a fixed
strictly compatible panel with positive slack, rejection probability instead
tends to zero as estimates converge. Our numerical trials establish persistence
over the tested budgets, not a universal asymptotic plateau, and an explicit
correction is what fixes the regime we tested.

\paragraph{The corrected version.} We instead evaluate the certificate on a
simultaneous one-sided lower confidence bound per cell, using the exact
binomial (Beta) quantile, Bonferroni-corrected across the panel's
$Z\times K$ cells:
\[
\mathrm{lo}_z(k) = \mathrm{Beta}^{-1}\big(\tfrac{\alpha}{ZK} \,\big|\, n_z(k),\, n_z - n_z(k) + 1\big).
\] The certificate
can then only report incompatibility when the data rule out compatibility at
the stated level. Its empirical false-positive rate has point estimates of at
most 0.05\% at every draw count tested, with 95\% Monte Carlo confidence
intervals reaching at most 0.13\% (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}, which
also gives the full grid, distinguishes these empirical rates from the
nominal $\alpha$ the correction targets, and explains the per-item and
family-wise regimes we report).

\paragraph{A per-model null.} Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} averages over answer
laws. It does not say whether \emph{this} model's naive incompatibility exceeds
chance, because the rate depends on the laws its items induce. So for each real
item we impose the null directly (one common law from the pooled
conditions, with that item's own per-condition disclosure rates) and resample,
giving the incompatibility count that model's items would produce with no
cross-condition structure.

\paragraph{Detection power against known violations.} The rates above are all
false-positive rates, since the population panel is compatible by
construction. Computing each simulated population's own $\gamma^*$ and
estimating power over the incompatible ones ($\gamma^*>0$), grouped by
magnitude (Appendix~\ref{app:calibration}), power rises with both violation
size and budget: moderate violations ($\gamma^*\in(0.05,0.10]$) reach 75\% at
256 draws and 95\% at 1{,}024, larger ones ($\gamma^*\in(0.10,0.20]$) 81\% at
128, well beyond the unanimous-opposition case \S\ref{sec:models} certifies. By
construction it certifies only what the budget can prove, silent on
near-boundary violations ($\gamma^*\le0.02$).

\section{Six checkpoints across four families}
\label{sec:models}

\paragraph{Setup.} We use all 480 non-neutral WinoGender template
instantiations (240 whose participant slot is the unnamed token
\textit{someone} and 240 naming a role), merged with Bergsma occupation
statistics to define the stereotype-consistent answer. Every checkpoint is
evaluated on every item, so nothing is sampled, drawing 16 stochastic
completions (temperature 0.8, independently seeded) under each of three
conditions. In the refusal\hy{}ablated condition, the model's refusal
direction is estimated by difference-in-means over paired refusing/complying
prompts and projected out of every block's residual stream
\citep{arditi2024refusal} before forcing an answer, an experimental
comparator rather than an established ground truth. \textit{Direct} is the
intact model permitted to abstain, and \textit{forced} is the intact model
required to choose, giving 23{,}040 generations per checkpoint and
138{,}240 in total, scored by one shared parser (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).
Checkpoints are Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024technical}, the full
OLMo-2-7B SFT$\to$DPO$\to$Instruct pipeline \citep{olmo2025furious} (its
intermediate post-training checkpoints are public), Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3
\citep{jiang2023mistral}, and Phi-3.5-mini-instruct \citep{abdin2024phi3}.
A 160-item screen motivated the full run, reported because several
conclusions changed with it. An indirect crosswise/randomized-response
condition, tried in three designs, collapsed to a constant answer in 90--100\%
of items at both 1.5B and 7B, excluded as a task-competence failure.

\input{tables/tab_certificate}

\paragraph{Naive incompatibility is mostly noise, but not entirely.} The
plug-in certificate rejects between 14\% (Mistral, 66 items) and 54\%
(OLMo-2-SFT, 260 items) of items, a nearly fourfold spread that looks
like a checkpoint property but mostly is not: the per-model null predicts
83--94\% of each checkpoint's rejections (92\% pooled,
Table~\ref{tab:certificate}). The remainder is real, exceeding every one of 200
null draws for all six checkpoints. A 160-item sample could not separate this
residue from noise ($p=0.06$--$0.11$), a third of the power. We read it as
genuine cross-condition disagreement spread across many items, each below the
per-item certification threshold.

\paragraph{The corrected certificate isolates genuine failures, and is
conservative.} Under correction four of six checkpoints return no violations.
Qwen2.5-7B returns 4 and Phi-3.5-mini 25, while the null produces zero
corrected rejections in every replication. Two features bound what this can
see. With 480 items and 16 draws, the corrected certificate can certify a
violation only when two conditions are unanimous (16/16) and opposed: two such
cells' lower bounds sum to 1.008, whereas a 15/16 cell has lower bound 0.414
and cannot. All 29 certified items meet this condition, and $\gamma^*=0.008$
is the smallest value the procedure can return, so the reported $\gamma^*$ is a
floor set by the draw budget, not the size of the disagreement, which is
total. At this budget and correction level, the certificate detects only
extreme disagreements. The aggregate excess over the constructed null in
Table~\ref{tab:certificate} does not quantify item-level power, which
\S\ref{sec:calibration} evaluates directly under controlled alternatives.

\paragraph{Which condition is at fault.} A violation count is not yet a
conclusion: the certificate says three conditions cannot share one answer law
but not which is responsible, and different culprits support opposite
readings. We classify each certified violation by the odd condition out. If the refusal-ablated comparator dissents while both intact
conditions agree, the ablation changed the answer rather than only the
willingness to give one, an \emph{instrument failure}. If \textit{direct}
dissents while \textit{forced} and the ablated condition agree, a
\emph{response-policy effect} changed the committed answer. Each diagnosis is
read off the disagreement pattern and names the condition at fault, though
agreement alone does not by itself prove instrument validity.

Of the 29 violations, 25 are instrument failures, 3 policy effects, and 1 a
forced-condition outlier (Table~\ref{tab:certificate}, every item in
Appendix~\ref{app:violations}). Phi-3.5-mini accounts for 22 of the
instrument failures. On two (\nolinkurl{scientist.someone.0.male},
\nolinkurl{machinist.child.1.female}) the ablated condition selects one category
16/16 and \emph{both} intact conditions the other. The diagnosis is sharper
than ``the ablation changed the answer'': Phi-3.5-mini \emph{never abstains} in
the direct condition (0 of 7{,}680 draws, even with the abstain option
offered), so it cannot be removing a refusal that never occurs. Qwen2.5-7B has 3 instrument failures, invisible in a 160-item sample (the full
pool moves five clean checkpoints to four), and 1 policy effect: on
\nolinkurl{counselor.patient.0.female} the ablated and
forced conditions agree (14 and 16 of 16 selecting the participant) while the
direct condition selects the occupation on 16 of 16, the correct antecedent.
Permitting abstention changed the committed answer, here for the better, and a
naive certificate's $\sim$20\% noise floor would hide it
(\S\ref{sec:calibration}).


\paragraph{Abstention leans toward unnamed participants, at different doses.}
WinoGender items whose participant slot is the unnamed token
\textit{someone} are harder to resolve than those naming a role, and we ask
whether abstention concentrates on them (Figure~\ref{fig:abstention}, Appendix~\ref{app:categories}). In every
checkpoint that abstains the lean is positive: $+22.8$ points for Qwen2.5
(26.7\% of draws on \textit{someone} items against 3.9\% on named), $+4.6$ for
Mistral, $+3.5$ for OLMo-2-DPO, $+2.9$ for OLMo-2-Instruct and $+1.0$ for
OLMo-2-SFT, four of five significant after Holm correction (not SFT).
Phi-3.5-mini never abstains.

Does the lean open a recovery gap on \textit{someone} items? Only for Qwen2.5:
the stratum difference in recovery error is $D=+0.099$ (95\% bootstrap CI $[+0.054,+0.145]$, permutation
$p<0.001$). For OLMo-2 and Mistral $|D|\le0.008$ with intervals of about
$\pm0.03$, and Phi-3.5-mini is $+0.035$ ($[-0.010,+0.080]$), not significant.
The ordering follows dose: the heaviest abstainer (15.3\% of draws) is the only
checkpoint where the gap appears, while the others abstain on 0--5\%, too
rarely. We read this as a difference in \emph{how much} a checkpoint abstains,
not as Qwen-specific.

\paragraph{Recovery-gap sensitivity to alternative instruments.} Only the
violation taxonomy and $D$ use the ablated condition, not the decomposition,
BBQ analysis, calibration, or abstention lean. Dropping the 4 items the
certificate flags for Qwen2.5 gives $D=+0.110$ ($[+0.067,+0.153]$). A
behaviour-derived direction (from the checkpoint's own abstained versus
answered items, with no instrument failures on Qwen2.5) strengthens it to
$D=+0.150$ ($[+0.106,+0.194]$, $p<0.001$). Mistral stays null under both
($|D|\le0.030$, $p\ge0.09$): the swap does not manufacture effects.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In the 32 published results we decomposed, reported bias reductions are
mostly a record of models learning to answer less often, and the metric that
rewards this is the quantity that destroys identification. First-party,
abstention is a property of the checkpoint and disposition of the social
category, so comparing checkpoints ranks them by abstention, a structure
surviving reworded prompts and extending to twelve checkpoints and six
languages, with Catalan's exception explained by the identity itself.
Re-eliciting under additional conditions is the right remedy, but its
plug-in form has a $\sim$21\% false-positive rate that persisted over the
tested sampling budgets. Our calibrated certificate localized 29 real
violations, sorted each by the condition at fault, and showed the recovery
gap strengthens under a cleaner instrument. For evaluations meeting these
assumptions, we recommend reporting coverage, committed-answer bias,
identified intervals, and calibrated compatibility results, distinguishing
detected incompatibility from causal diagnosis.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item The published-table decomposition (\S\ref{sec:decomposition}) covers the
  32 pairs of one study under one harness, and the first-party replication
  (\S\ref{sec:firstparty}) establishes the phenomenon independently of it.
  Applying the identity across the wider literature requires papers to report
  ambiguous-split accuracy and bias score together, which \S\ref{sec:firstparty}
  found is not yet standard.
\item The decomposition takes the reported ambiguous score to be BBQ's official
  $s_{\text{AMB}}$ and ambiguous accuracy to be the \textsc{unknown} rate, as
  the benchmark defines them. The authors' per-item outputs are not released,
  so this could not be checked item by item.
\item Conditions use one prompt template each (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}) at
  one temperature. The BBQ structure holds under a reworded prompt
  (\S\ref{sec:firstparty}), while absolute abstention levels are
  prompt-dependent, and the WinoGender results use one template.
\item At 480 items and 16 draws the corrected certificate certifies unanimous,
  opposed disagreement, the price of its false-positive rate of at most 0.1\%.
  The null residue in Table~\ref{tab:certificate} measures what it leaves
  uncertified, and more draws per item raise its power.
\item The generic refusal direction is not bias\hy{}specific. The
  refusal-ablated condition is the dissenting condition in 22 certified
  violations for Phi-3.5-mini and 3 for Qwen2.5, a pattern consistent with
  instrument failure. A behaviour\hy{}derived direction removes the
  instrument failures of Qwen2.5 and strengthens its recovery gap
  (\S\ref{sec:models}).
  It needs at least 8 abstentions to build, which holds for Qwen2.5
  (58 abstained) and Mistral (19) but not for Phi-3.5-mini or the three OLMo-2
  checkpoints (0 abstained), and on Mistral it gives
  4 instrument failures and 1 outlier where the generic direction gave none. A general-purpose repair of
  the instrument is therefore open.
\item The abstention lean was analysed after the recovery-gap result and is
  observational. The dose reading is not tested by intervening on abstention,
  and it rests on five abstaining checkpoints from three families.
\item The \S\ref{sec:models} refusal-ablation results use WinoGender only,
  English only, one prompt template, and WinoGender carries template defects
  documented by \citet{gautam2024winopron}. The decomposition and first-party
  structure (\S\ref{sec:multilingual}) extend to six languages and twelve
  checkpoints, but on BBQ alone. The tools are benchmark-agnostic, and
  extending \S\ref{sec:models}'s instrument work to WinoGender in other
  languages, and to open-ended generation, is direct future work. Catalan is
  tested through one independently-built dataset, so a Catalan-language
  effect is not separable there from a dataset-construction effect.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Ethics Statement}

This paper argues that a class of reported debiasing results overstates what
was achieved. We want to be careful about what follows from that and what does
not.

It does not follow that the interventions we decompose are harmful or that
their authors did anything improper. They report accuracy and bias score
exactly as the benchmark specifies, and BBQ's scaling of the ambiguous score
by the commitment rate is a deliberate design choice its authors motivate
explicitly. Our claim is about what the resulting number can support, not
about anyone's conduct. Nor does it follow that abstention is bad: declining
to answer an under-specified question about a person's group membership is
often the appropriate behavior, and a model that learns to do so has
learned something worth having. The problem we identify is narrower and
purely epistemic. The same movement that improves the score destroys the
evidence needed to check whether the underlying disposition changed, so the
metric cannot distinguish a model that stopped preferring the stereotype from
one that merely stopped saying so.

That distinction has practical stakes, not only in principle. Qwen2.5-7B
posts a near-perfect first-party baseline score ($s_{\text{AMB}}=0.033$,
Table~\ref{tab:firstparty}) while its own stereotype-aligned rate $\theta$,
measured on the same items under forced choice, is 62.2\%. A deployment
decision made on the strength of the published-style score alone would be
relying on a number compatible, by the width of its own identified interval,
with a meaningfully stereotype-leaning disposition underneath. Our
recommendation is therefore additive rather than prohibitive: report the
abstention rate and the committed-answer bias alongside the headline score,
and report the identified interval, so readers can see how much the
evaluation actually pins down.

We work exclusively with existing public benchmarks (BBQ, WinoGender) and
public model checkpoints. No new human-subjects data was collected. These
benchmarks encode a US-centric, largely binary treatment of gender and a
specific inventory of social categories, and WinoGender carries documented
template defects \citep{gautam2024winopron}. Results here should not be read
as characterizing bias in any absolute sense, nor as transferring to other
languages, cultures, or deployment settings. Finally, our experiments
deliberately elicit stereotype-aligned outputs from models by removing their
ability to decline. This is done to measure disposition, and we release
per-item outputs for verification, not as a technique for eliciting harmful
content in deployment.

\paragraph{Symmetric decomposition.} The identity in \S\ref{sec:decomposition}

\section{Calibration details}
\label{app:calibration}

Table~\ref{tab:calibration} gives the full false-positive grid behind

\emph{family-wise} regime additionally divides $\alpha$ by the number of items,
holding a 0.05 rate across a whole screen. It controls family-wise error
across the screen but becomes more stringent per item as the screen grows,
reducing sensitivity at a fixed per-item sampling budget. Consequently, fewer
detected violations need not imply greater compatibility. The
\emph{per-item} regime holds per-item stringency constant in screen size. We
report both so that compatibility rates cannot be an artifact of how many
items were run.

Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} calibrate specificity against a compatible null.

\texttt{mix}$\sim$U$[0,0.4]$ on the same empirically resampled disclosure,

\S\ref{sec:calibration}: its apparent power is not evidence the certificate can
trust, which is why the corrected test's power should be read against its
near-zero false-positive floor.

\section{Proof of the compatibility characterization}
\label{app:proof}

\S\ref{sec:certificate} verifies \texttt{compatible} and $\gamma^*$
numerically, against an independent exponential-size formulation and against
closed forms at $Z=2,K=2$. This appendix proves both exactly, for general
$Z$ and $K$, so the certificate rests on a proof rather than agreement on
test cases.

\paragraph{Setup.} Condition $z$ discloses a substantive category with the
sub-probabilities $p_z(1),\dots,p_z(K)$, with $\sum_k p_z(k) \le 1$. The
missing mass is non-disclosure. The model under test is: a single answer law
$Y\in\{1,\dots,K\}$, shared across all $Z$ conditions, together with an
arbitrary, condition-specific, answer-\emph{dependent} disclosure rule, could
have produced every $p_z(\cdot)$ at once. \texttt{compatible} asks whether
this model is consistent with the observed panel.

\begin{lemma}[Agreement under fixed marginals]
\label{lem:agreement}
Let $Y_1,\dots,Y_Z$ be any random variables on $\{1,\dots,K\}$ with
$P(Y_z=k)=q_z(k)$. Over every joint coupling of $Y_1,\dots,Y_Z$ consistent
with these marginals,
\[
\max_{\text{couplings}} P(Y_1=\cdots=Y_Z) = \sum_{k=1}^K \min_z q_z(k),
\]
and this value is attained.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Upper bound.} The events $\{Y_1=\cdots=Y_Z=k\}$ for $k=1,\dots,K$ are
disjoint, and $\{Y_1=\cdots=Y_Z=k\}\subseteq\{Y_z=k\}$ for every $z$, so
$P(Y_1=\cdots=Y_Z=k)\le\min_z q_z(k)$. Summing over $k$ gives the bound.

\emph{Attainment.} Write $m(k)=\min_z q_z(k)$ and $M=\sum_k m(k)\in[0,1]$.
If $M=1$, take $Y_1=\cdots=Y_Z=W$ for $W\sim m/M$ when $M>0$. (When
$M=0$ the bound is $0$, and any coupling attains it.) If $M<1$, the residual masses
$r_z(k)=q_z(k)-m(k)\ge0$ each sum to $1-M$. Mix: with probability $M$ draw a
single $W\sim m(\cdot)/M$ and set every $Y_z=W$. With the complementary
probability $1-M$, draw $Y_1,\dots,Y_Z$ \emph{independently}, each from
$r_z(\cdot)/(1-M)$. By construction $P(Y_z=k)=M\cdot m(k)/M + (1-M)\cdot
r_z(k)/(1-M) = q_z(k)$, so the marginals are exactly right, and
$P(Y_1=\cdots=Y_Z)\ge P(\text{shared branch})=M$. Combined with the upper
bound this coupling attains $P(Y_1=\cdots=Y_Z)=M$ exactly.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Compatibility]
\label{prop:compatible}
A single shared answer law with arbitrary condition-specific, answer
-dependent disclosure reproduces $p_1,\dots,p_Z$ exactly if and only if
$\sum_k \max_z p_z(k) \le 1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
($\Leftarrow$) Set $\pi(k)=\max_z p_z(k)$ for every $k$, and, if
$\sum_k\pi(k)<1$, add the deficit to $\pi(1)$ so $\pi$ sums to exactly $1$,
giving a valid law for $Y$. For each $z$, let the disclosure rate at
category $k$ be $d_z(k)=p_z(k)/\pi(k)$ when $\pi(k)>0$ (and, since
$p_z(k)\le\max_{z'}p_{z'}(k)=\pi(k)$, this lies in $[0,1]$). When $\pi(k)=0$,
every $p_z(k)=0$ and $d_z(k)$ is unconstrained. Disclosing category $k$ under
condition $z$ with probability $d_z(k)$ given $Y=k$ reproduces
$P(Y=k,\text{disclosed})=\pi(k)d_z(k)=p_z(k)$ exactly, for every $z,k$.

($\Rightarrow$) If such a $Y\sim\pi$ and disclosure rule exist, then for every
$z,k$, $p_z(k)=\pi(k)\cdot d_z(k)\le\pi(k)$ since $d_z(k)\le1$. Hence
$\pi(k)\ge\max_z p_z(k)$ for every $k$, and $1=\sum_k\pi(k)\ge\sum_k\max_z
p_z(k)$.
\end{proof}

This is exactly \texttt{compatible} in \S\ref{sec:certificate}, with
$\gamma^*=0$ the special case of Proposition~\ref{prop:necessary} below.

\begin{proposition}[Sharpness of $\gamma^*$]
\label{prop:necessary}
Define, over all $(q_1,\dots,q_Z)$ with $q_z(k)\ge p_z(k)$ and
$\sum_k q_z(k)=1$ for every $z$, and all couplings of random variables with
these marginals,
\[
\gamma^\dagger \;=\; 1 \,-\, \max_{q_1,\dots,q_Z}\ \max_{\text{couplings}}\
P(Y_1=\cdots=Y_Z).
\]
Then $\gamma^\dagger$ equals the linear program of \S\ref{sec:certificate},
{\small
\[
\gamma^* = 1-\max_t\ \Big\{ \textstyle\sum_k t_k \ :\
\textstyle\sum_k\max(t_k,p_z(k))\le1\ \forall z \Big\} .
\]}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:agreement}, for fixed completions $q_1,\dots,q_Z$ the best
achievable agreement is $\sum_k\min_z q_z(k)$, so
$1-\gamma^\dagger=\max_{q_1,\dots,q_Z}\sum_k\min_z q_z(k)$ subject to
$q_z(k)\ge p_z(k)$, $\sum_k q_z(k)=1$.

\emph{$1-\gamma^\dagger \le$ LP optimum.} Let $q_1^*,\dots,q_Z^*$ attain
$1-\gamma^\dagger$ and set $t_k=\min_z q_z^*(k)$. Since $q_z^*(k)\ge p_z(k)$
and $q_z^*(k)\ge t_k$ by definition of the minimum, $q_z^*(k)\ge\max(t_k,
p_z(k))$ for every $z,k$. Summing over $k$, $\sum_k\max(t_k,p_z(k))\le
\sum_k q_z^*(k)=1$ for every $z$, so $t$ is LP-feasible, and $\sum_k t_k =
\sum_k\min_z q_z^*(k) = 1-\gamma^\dagger$.

\emph{LP optimum $\le 1-\gamma^\dagger$.} Let $t^*$ attain the LP optimum and
set $q_z(k)=\max(t_k^*,p_z(k))$ for every $z,k$. By LP feasibility $\sum_k
q_z(k)\le1$, and any deficit can be added to $q_z(1)$ (which only increases
$q_z(1)\ge\max(t_1^*,p_z(1))$, so feasibility of the completion is preserved)
to make $q_z$ sum to exactly $1$. Each such $q_z$ satisfies $q_z(k)\ge
p_z(k)$ (a valid completion) and $q_z(k)\ge t_k^*$, so $\min_z q_z(k)\ge
t_k^*$ and $\sum_k\min_z q_z(k)\ge\sum_k t_k^*$. Since $1-\gamma^\dagger$ is
the maximum of $\sum_k\min_z q_z(k)$ over all valid completions, $1-
\gamma^\dagger\ge\sum_k t_k^*=$ LP optimum.

Both inequalities give $1-\gamma^\dagger=$ LP optimum, i.e.\
$\gamma^\dagger=\gamma^*$.
\end{proof}

$\gamma^\dagger$ is by construction the sharp minimum probability that the
$Z$ conditions disagree, over every population consistent with the observed
disclosed masses and every coupling of that population: no relaxation of the
common-answer-law model can do better than $\gamma^*$, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:agreement}'s construction exhibits a population and coupling
that achieves exactly $\gamma^*$ disagreement. Specializing to $\gamma^*=0$
recovers Proposition~\ref{prop:compatible} exactly: $\gamma^*=0$ forces
$\sum_k t_k^*=1$, so the common mass $t^*$ is itself a single distribution
that completes every condition, which is exactly compatibility. The two
results are therefore one family, not two separate claims verified only
numerically.

\end{footnotesize}
A response is scored \emph{abstained} if it is empty or contains ``cannot'' or
``determine''. Otherwise it is \emph{answered} with the occupation if it contains the
occupation string, or with the participant if it contains that string, and
is otherwise \emph{unparsed}. Unparsed draws are retained and treated as
non-disclosure in the certificate, an operational category that combines
explicit abstention with parsing failures and should not be read as a pure
measure of abstention. The same rule scores every condition and every
checkpoint.

\paragraph{Parser validation.} We audited the BBQ parser
(\S\ref{sec:decomposition}) against its saved raw text: a stratified sample
of 161 draws (6 checkpoints $\times$ 2 conditions $\times$ 3 outcomes
-- answered, abstained, unparsed) checked against an independently
implemented classifier using different logic (whole-response fuzzy text
matching plus a separate abstention-cue list, rather than the parser's
prefix matching). The two agreed on all 161, and a separate manual read of 32
cases (20 answered, 12 abstained) confirmed the assigned option against the
option text on every one. The 17 unparsed cases are generations truncated by
the token budget before any option is named, correctly scored as
non-disclosure rather than a parsing gap. WinoGender's per-item files
(\S\ref{sec:models}) retain only aggregated per-item counts from that run,
so we validated the shared parser directly with a fresh sample: 3,840 draws
(6 checkpoints $\times$ 80 items $\times$ 4 draws $\times$ 2 conditions,
\emph{direct} and \emph{forced}) scored by the same function and
cross-checked against an independently implemented classifier (fuzzy
whole-response matching plus a separate abstention-cue list). The two agreed
on 3,838/3,840 (99.95\%). A manual read of both disagreements and of all 53
unparsed draws confirmed the shared parser's call was correct in every case:
the two disagreements are the same hallucinated word (``barrister'') that
superficially resembles an option (``bartender'') and fooled only the
independent classifier's fuzzy match, and every unparsed draw is a genuine
non-match, either a model naming neither option or substituting a bare
pronoun when the participant string is the generic ``someone''.

\paragraph{BBQ instruction prefix.} The \emph{instructed} condition prepends
``Please ensure your answer is unbiased and does not rely on stereotypes about
gender, race, religion, age, or any other social group. If the context does not
contain enough information to answer, you should say so rather than guessing.''
to the baseline prompt. The forced stereotype-aligned rate $\theta$ is read from
the first-token logits of the two option letters, so every item is scored. The
option order in the forced condition uses a fixed seed.
